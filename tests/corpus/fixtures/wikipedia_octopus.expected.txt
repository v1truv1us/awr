Anoctopus(pl.:octopusesoroctopodes[a][37]) is a soft-bodied, eight-limbedmollusc[38]of
theorder[39]Octopoda(/ɒkˈtɒpədə/[40],ok-TOP-ə-də[41][3][42]). The order
consists of some 300species[43]and is grouped within the classCephalopoda[44]withsquids[45],cuttlefish[46],
andnautiloids[47]. Like other cephalopods, an octopus isbilaterally symmetric[48]with
two eyes and abeaked[49]mouth at the centre point of the eight limbs.[b][50]An
octopus can radically deform its shape, enabling it to squeeze through small
gaps. They trail their appendages behind them as they swim backwards. Thesiphon[51]is
used forrespiration[52]andlocomotion[53](bywater jet propulsion[54]).
Octopuses have a complex nervous system and excellent sight, and are among the
most intelligent and behaviourally diverseinvertebrates[55].
Octopuses inhabit variousocean[56]habitats, includingcoral reefs[57],pelagic[58]waters,
and theseabed[59]; some live in theintertidal zone[60]and others atabyssal
depths[61]. Most species grow quickly, mature early, and are short-lived. In
most species, the male uses a specially-adapted arm to deliver sperm directly
into the female's mantle cavity, after which he becomessenescent[62]and dies,
while the female deposits fertilised eggs in a den and cares for them until
they hatch, after which she also dies. They are predators and huntcrustaceans[63],bivalves[64],gastropods[65]and
fish. Strategies to defend themselves against their own predators include
expellingink[66],camouflage[67], andthreat displays[68], the ability to jet
quickly through the water and hide, and deceit. All octopuses arevenomous[69],
but only theblue-ringed octopuses[70]are known to be deadly to humans.
Octopuses appear inmythology[71]as sea monsters such as thekraken[72]of Norway
and theAkkorokamui[73]of theAinu[74], and possibly theGorgons[75]ofancient
Greece[76]. A battle with an octopus appears inVictor Hugo[77]'s bookToilers
of the Sea[78]. Octopuses appear in Japaneseshunga[79]erotic art. They are
eaten and considered a delicacy by humans in many parts of the world,
especially theMediterranean[80]and Asia.

---------------------------

ofὀκτώ[87](oktō, 'eight') andπούς[88](pous, 'foot'), itself a
variant form ofὀκτάπους[89], a word used for example byAlexander of
Tralles[90](c. 525–c. 605).[5][91][6][92][7][93]
The standardpluralised[94]form ofoctopusin English isoctopuses;[8][95]the
Ancient Greek pluralὀκτώποδες,octopodes(/ɒkˈtɒpədiːz/[96]), has
also been used historically.[9][97]The alternative pluraloctopiis usually
considered etymologically incorrect, because itwrongly assumes[98]thatoctopusis
a Latinsecond-declension[99]-us[100]noun or adjective when, in either Greek or
Latin, it is athird-declension[101]noun.[10][102][11][103]Historically, the
first plural to commonly appear in English language sources, in the early 19th
century, is the Latinate formoctopi,[12][104]followed by the English formoctopusesin
the latter half of the same century. The Hellenic plural is roughly
contemporary in usage, although it is also the rarest.[13][105]
A Dictionary of Modern English Usage[106]by H. W. Fowler states that the only
acceptable plural in English isoctopuses, thatoctopiis misconceived, andoctopodespedantic[107];[14][108][15][109][16][110]the
last is nonetheless used frequently enough to be acknowledged by thedescriptivist[111]Merriam-Webster
11th Collegiate DictionaryandWebster's New World College Dictionary.[17][112]TheOxford
English Dictionary[113]listsoctopuses,octopi, andoctopodes, in that order,

based on a misunderstanding.[18][114]TheNew Oxford American Dictionary[115](3rd
Edition, 2010) listsoctopusesas the only acceptable pluralisation, and

Anatomy and physiology
----------------------

Size

See also:Cephalopod size[117][Captured specimen of a giant octopus][119]Agiant
Pacific octopus[120]at Echizen Matsushima Aquarium, JapanThegiant Pacific
octopus[121](Enteroctopus dofleini) is often cited as the largest octopus
species. Adults usually weigh 10–50 kg (22–110 lb), with an arm span of
up to 4.8 m (16 ft).[20][122]The largest specimen of this species to be
scientifically documented reached a live mass of 71 kg (157 lb).[21][123]Much
larger sizes have been claimed:[22][124]one specimen was recorded as 600 lb
(270 kg) with an arm span of 32 ft (9.8 m).[23][125]A carcass of theseven-arm
octopus[126],Haliphron atlanticus[127], weighed 61 kg (134 lb) and was
estimated to have had a live mass of 75 kg (165 lb).[24][128][25][129]The
smallest species isOctopus wolfi[130], which is around 2.5 cm (1 in) and
weighs less than 1 g (0.035 oz).[26][131]
External characteristics

The octopus has an elongated body that isbilaterally symmetrical[132]along its
dorso-ventral (back to belly) axis; the head andfoot[133]are on the ventral
side, but act as the anterior (front). The head contains both the mouth and
the brain.[27][134]: 343–344 The mouth has a sharpchitinous[135]beak[136]and
is surrounded by and underneath the foot, which evolved into flexible,
prehensilelimbs[137], known as "arms", which are attached to each other near
their base by a webbed structure.[27][138]: 343–344 [28][139]: 40–41 [29][140]: 13–15 The
arms can be described based on side and sequence position (such as L1, R1, L2,
R2) and divide into four pairs.[30][141]: 12 The two rear appendages are
generally used to walk on the sea floor, while the other six are used to
forage for food.[31][142]The bulbous and hollowmantle[143]is fused to the back
of the head and contains most of the vital organs.[29][144]: 13–15 [28][145]: 40–41 The
mantle also has a cavity with muscular walls and a pair of gills; it is
connected to the exterior by a funnel orsiphon[146].[27][147]: 343–344 [32][148]
[Schematic of external anatomy][150]Diagram of octopus from side, with gills,
funnel, eye,ocellus[151](eyespot), web, arms, suckers,hectocotylus[152]andligula[153]labelled.The
skin consists of a thinepidermis[154]with mucous cells and sensory cells and a
fibrousdermis[155]made ofcollagen[156]and containing various cells that allow
colour change.[27][157]: 362 Most of the body is made of soft tissue,
allowing it to squeeze through tiny gaps; even the larger species can pass
through a gap little more than 2.5 cm (1 in) in diameter.[28][158]: 40–41 Lacking
skeletal support, the arms work asmuscular hydrostats[159]and feature
longitudinal, transverse, and circular muscles around a central axial nerve.
They can squash and stretch, coil at any place in any direction or stiffen.[33][160][34][161]
The interior surfaces of the arms are covered with circular, adhesive suckers.
The suckers allow the octopus to secure itself in place or to handle objects.
Each sucker is typically circular and bowl-like and has two distinct parts: an
outer disc-shapedinfundibulum[162]and an inner cup-likeacetabulum[163], both
of which are thick muscles covered inconnective tissue[164]. Achitinous[165]cuticle
lines the outer surface. When a sucker attaches to a surface, the orifice
between the two structures is sealed and the infundibulum flattens. Muscle
contractions allow for attachment and detachment.[35][166][36][167][33][168]Each
of the eight arms senses and responds to light, allowing the octopus to
control its limbs even if its head is obscured.[37][169]
[A stubby round sea-creature with short ear-like fins][171]A finnedGrimpoteuthis[172]species
with its atypical octopus body planThe cranium has twocartilaginous[173]capsules
each containing one large eye, which resembles those of fish. Thecornea[174]is
formed from atranslucent[175]epidermal layer; the slit-shapedpupil[176]forms a
hole in theiris[177]just behind the cornea. The lens hangs behind the pupil;
photoreceptiveretinal cells[178]line the back. The pupil can expand and
contract; a retinal pigment screens incident light in bright conditions.[27][179]: 360–361 
Some species differ in form from the typical body shape.Basal[180]species, theCirrina[181],
have gelatinous bodies with twofins[182]located above the eyes, aninternal
shell[183]and mostly webbed arms that are lined with fleshy papillae orcirri[184]underneath.[38][185][39][186]
Circulatory system

Octopuses have a closedcirculatory system[187], in which the blood remains
inside blood vessels. They have three hearts; a systemic or main heart that
circulates blood around the body and two branchial or gill hearts that pump it
through the two gills. The systemic heart becomes inactive when the animal is
swimming. Thus, the octopus loses energy quickly and mostly crawls.[30][188]: 19–20, 31–35 [28][189]: 42–43 Octopus
blood contains thecopper[190]-rich proteinhaemocyanin[191]to transport oxygen.
This makes the bloodviscous[192]and it requires great pressure to pump it
around the body;blood pressures[193]can surpass 75 mmHg (10 kPa).[30][194]: 31–35 [28][195]: 42–43 [40][196]In
cold conditions with low oxygen levels, haemocyanin transports oxygen more
efficiently thanhaemoglobin[197].[41][198]The haemocyanin is dissolved in theblood
plasma[199]instead of carried within blood cells and gives the blood a bluish
colour.[30][200]: 31–35 [28][201]: 42–43 [29][202]: 22 
The systemic heart has muscular contractile walls and consists of a singleventricle[203]and
twoatria[204], which attach it to each of the two gills. The blood vessels
consist of arteries, capillaries and veins and are lined with a cellularendothelium[205]unlike
that of most otherinvertebrates[206]. The blood circulates through theaorta[207]and
capillary system, to thevenae cavae[208], after which the blood is pumped
through the gills by the branchial hearts and back to the main heart. Much of
the venous system is contractile, which helps circulate the blood.[27][209]: 358 
Respiration

[An octopus on the seabed, its siphon protruding near its eye][211]Octopus
with open siphon. The siphon is used for respiration, waste disposal and
discharging ink.Respiration[212]involves drawing water into the mantle cavity
through an aperture, passing it through the gills, and expelling it through
the siphon. Ingress is achieved by contraction of radial muscles in the mantle
wall, and flapper valves shut when strong, circular muscles expel the water
through the siphon.[42][213]Extensive connective tissue lattices support the
respiratory muscles and allow them to inflate the respiratory chamber.[30][214]: 24–26 Thelamella[215]structure
of the gills allows for high oxygen uptake, up to 65% in water at 20 °C
(68 °F).[43][216]Respiration can also play a role in locomotion, as an
octopus can propel its body shooting water out of the siphon.[30][217]: 18 [40][218]
The thin skin absorbs additional oxygen. When resting, around 41% of oxygen
absorption is through the skin, reduced to 33% when the octopus swims, despite
the amount of oxygen absorption increasing as water flows over the body. When
it is resting after a meal, skin absorption can drop to 3%.[44][219]
Digestion and excretion

The digestive system begins with thebuccal mass[220]which consists of the
mouth with the beak, thepharynx[221],radula[222]and salivary glands.[30][223]: 71–74 The
radula is serrated and made ofchitin[224].[28][225]: 40–41 Food is
broken down and is forced into theesophagus[226]by two lateral extensions of
the esophageal side walls in addition to the radula. From there it is
transferred to thegastrointestinal tract[227], which is mostly suspended from
the roof of the mantle cavity. The tract consists of acrop[228], where the
food is stored; a stomach, where it is mixed with other gut material; acaecum[229]where
the food is separated into particles and liquids and which absorbs fats; thedigestive
gland[230], where liver cells break down and absorb the fluid and become
"brown bodies"; and the intestine, where the built-up waste is turned into
faecal ropes by secretions and ejected out of the funnel via therectum[231].[30][232]: 75–79 
Duringosmoregulation[233], fluid is added to thepericardia[234]of the
branchial hearts. The octopus has twonephridia[235](equivalent to vertebrate
kidneys) that are associated with the branchial hearts; these and their
associated ducts connect the pericardial cavities with the mantle cavity. Each
branch of thevena cava[236]has renal appendages that pass over the thin-walled
nephridium before reaching the branchial heart. Urine is created in the
pericardial cavity, and is altered by excretion, of mostly ammonia, and
absorption from the renal appendages, as it is passed along the associated
duct and through the nephridiopore into the mantle cavity.[27][237]: 358–359 
Acommon octopus[238](Octopus vulgaris) moving around. Its nervous system
allows the arms to move with some autonomy.Nervous system and senses

Octopuses and their relatives have a more expansive and complexnervous system[239]than
other invertebrates, containing over 500 millionneurons[240], around the same
as a dog.[45][241][46][242][47][243]One part is localised in the brain,
contained in a cartilaginous capsule. Two-thirds of the neurons are in the
nerve cords of its arms. This allows their arms to perform actions with a
degree of independence.[48][244]Learning mainly occurs in the brain, while
arms make decisions independently when supplied with information.[49][245]A
severed arm can still move and respond to stimuli.[50][246]Unlike in many
other animals, including other mollusks, the movement of octopuses and their
relatives are not organised in their brains via internalsomatotopic maps[247]of
their bodies.[51][248]Octopuses have the samejumping genes[249]that are active
in the human brain, implying anevolutionary convergence[250]at molecular
level.[52][251]
[Close up of an octopus showing its eye and an arm with suckers][253]Eye ofcommon
octopus[254]Like other cephalopods, octopuses have camera-like eyes.[45][255]Colour
vision[256]appears to vary from species to species, for example, it is present
inA. aegina[257]but absent inO. vulgaris[258].[53][259]Opsins[260]in the skin
respond to different wavelengths of light and help the animals choose a
colouration that matches the surroundings and camouflages them;chromatophores[261]in
the skin can respond to light independently of the eyes.[54][262][55][263]An
alternative hypothesis is thatcephalopod eyes[264]in species that only have a
singlephotoreceptor protein[265]may usechromatic aberration[266]to turn
monochromatic vision into colour vision, though this lowers image quality.
This would explain pupils shaped like the letter "U", the letter "W", or adumbbell[267],
as well as the need for colourful mating displays.[56][268]
Attached to the optic capsules are two organs calledstatocysts[269](sac-like
structures containing a mineralised mass and sensitive hairs), that allow the
octopus to sense the orientation of its body, relative to both gravity and
time (angular acceleration[270]). Anautonomic[271]response keeps the octopus's
eyes oriented so that the pupil is always horizontal.[27][272]: 360–361 Octopuses
may also use the statocyst to hear. The common octopus can hear sounds between
400 Hz and 1000 Hz, and hears best at 600 Hz.[57][273]
Octopuses have an excellentsomatosensory system[274]. Their suction cups are
equipped withchemoreceptors[275]so they cantaste[276]what they touch.[58][277]Octopus
arms move easily because the sensors recognise octopus skin and prevent
self-attachment.[59][278]Octopuses appear to have poorproprioceptive[279]sense
and must see their arms to keep track of their position.[60][280][61][281]
Ink sac

Theink sac[282]is located under the digestive gland. A gland attached to the
sac produces theink[283], and the sac holds it. The sac is close enough to the
funnel for the octopus to shoot out the ink with a water jet. As the animal
begins to shoot, the ink passes through glands which mix it with mucus and it
leaves the funnel as a thick, dark blob, which helps the animal to escape from
a predator.[29][284]: 107 The mainpigment[285]in the ink ismelanin[286],
which gives it its black colour.[62][287]Cirrate octopuses usually lack the
ink sac.[38][288]
Life cycle
----------

Reproduction

[Drawing of a male octopus with one large arm ending in the sexual apparatus][290]Adult
maleTremoctopus violaceus[291]withhectocotylus[292]Octopuseshave two sexes[293]and
have only one gonad (testis[294]in males andovary[295]in females) located
posteriorly. The gonad depositsgametes[296]into an adjacent cavity called the
gonocoel. Agonoduct[297]bridges the gonocoel with themantle cavity[298].[27][299]: 363–365 Anoptic
gland[300]creates hormones that cause the octopus to mature and age and
stimulate gamete production. The timing of reproduction and lifespan depends
on environmental conditions such as temperature, light and nutrition, which
trigger the gland.[29][301]: 147 [63][302]The male has a specialised arm
called ahectocotylus[303]which it uses to transferspermatophores[304](packets
of sperm) into the female's mantle cavity.[27][305]: 363–365 The
hectocotylus inOctopusis usually the R3 arm, which has a spoon-shaped
depression and a suckerless tip.[30][306]: 12–14 [27][307]: 363–365 Fertilisation
may occur in the mantle cavity or in the surrounding water.[27][308]: 363–365 
Reproduction has been studied in some species. In thegiant Pacific octopus[309],
courtship includes changes in skin texture and colour, mostly in the male. The
male may cling to the top or side of the female or position himself beside
her. There is some speculation that he may first use his hectocotylus to
remove any spermatophore or sperm already present in the female. He picks up a
spermatophore from his spermatophoric sac with the hectocotylus, inserts it
into the female's mantle cavity, and deposits it in the correct location in
the opening of the oviduct. Two spermatophores are transferred in this way;
these are about one metre (yard) long, and the empty ends may protrude from
the female's mantle.[64][310]A complex hydraulic mechanism releases the sperm
from the spermatophore.[27][311]: 363–365 
[A female octopus underneath hanging strings of her eggs][313]Female giant
Pacific octopus guarding strings of eggsThe eggs have large yolks;cleavage[314](division)
is relatively shallow and agerminal disc[315]develops at the pole. Duringgastrulation[316],
the disc surrounds the yolk, forming a yolk sac, which eventually forms part
of the gut. The embryo forms as the dorsal side of the disc grows upward, with
a shell gland, gills, mantle and eyes on its dorsal side. The arms and funnel
form on the ventral side of the disc, with the former moving upward to
surround the mouth. The embryo consumes the yolk during development.[27][317]: 363–365 
Over a month after mating, giant Pacific octopuses lay eggs. The species can
lay 180,000 eggs in a single clutch, whileO. rubescens[318]clutches host up to
45,000 eggs whileO. vulgarisclutches can include 500,000 eggs.[65][319]: 75 Fertilised
octopus eggs are laid as strings within a shelter.[64][320][29][321]: 26 Female
giant Pacific octopuses nurture and protect their eggs for five months (160
days) until they hatch.[64][322]In colder waters, such as those offAlaska[323],
it may take up to ten months for the eggs to completely develop.[65][324]: 74 In
theargonaut[325](paper nautilus), the female is much larger than the male. She
secretes a thin shell shaped like acornucopia[326], in which the eggs are
deposited and in which she also resides and broods the young while swimming.[29][327]: 26, 141 
[A microscopic view of a small round-bodied transparent animal with very short arms][329]Octopusparalarva[330],
aplanktonic[331]hatchlingMost young octopuses hatch asparalarvae[332],[27][333]: 363–365 Octopuslarvae
in particular areplanktonic[334]for weeks or months. Larvae feed on shrimp,
isopods andamphipods[335], eventually settling on the ocean floor to mature.[30][336]: 178 Species
that produce larger eggs instead hatch as benthic animals similar to the
adults.[65][337]: 74–75 These include thesouthern blue-ringed[338],Caribbean
reef[339],California two-spot[340]andEledone moschata[341].[66][342]
Lifespan

Octopuses have short lifespans, living up to four years.[29][343]: 17 The
lifecycles of some species finish in less than half a year.[28][344]: 152 For
most octopuses, the ultimate life stage issenescence[345]. It is the breakdown
of cellular function without repair or replacement. It may last from weeks to
a few months at most. Males senesce after maturity, while for females, it
comes after they lay an egg clutch. During senescence, an octopus does not
feed, quickly weakens, and becomes sluggish. Lesions begin to form and the
octopus literally degenerates. They may die of starvation or get picked off by
predators.[67][346]Senescence is triggered by the optic glands and
experimental removal of them after spawning was found to extend their
lifecycle and activity.[68][347]
Distribution and habitat
------------------------

[An octopus nearly hidden in a crack in some coral][349]Octopus cyaneain Kona,
HawaiiOctopuses inhabit every ocean, with species adapted to manyhabitats[350].
As juveniles, common octopuses inhabit shallowtide pools[351]. The Hawaiian
day octopus (Octopus cyanea[352]) lives on coral reefs, whileargonauts[353]float
inpelagic waters[354].Abdopus aculeatus[355]is a near-shore species and can be
found inseagrass[356]beds. Some species can survive in deeper environments.
The spoon-armed octopus (Bathypolypus arcticus[357]) can live 1,000 m
(3,300 ft) deep, andVulcanoctopus hydrothermalis[358]lives in depths of
2,000 m (6,600 ft) aroundhydrothermal vents[359].[29][360]: 13–15 Species
such asMegaleledone setebos[361]andPareledone charcoti[362], can survive in
the waters of the Antarctic, which reach −1.8 °C (29 °F).[41][363]No
species are known to live in fresh water.[69][364]
Thecirrate[365]species are often free-swimming and live in deep-water
habitats.[39][366]Although several species live atbathyal[367]andabyssal[368]depths,
only a single indisputable record documents their presence in thehadal zone[369];
a species ofGrimpoteuthis[370](dumbo octopus) photographed at 6,957 m
(22,825 ft).[70][371]
Behaviour and ecology
---------------------

Octopuses are mostly solitary[29][372]: 17, 134 though a few are known
to live in groups and interact regularly, usually in the context ofdominance[373]and
reproductive competition. This is likely the result of abundant food supplies
combined with fewer den sites.[71][374]TheLarger Pacific striped octopus[375]has
been described as particularly social, living in groups of up to 40.[72][376][73][377]Octopuses
hide in dens, which are typically crevices in rocky or other hard structures,
including man-made ones. Small species may use abandoned shells and bottles.[29][378]: 69, 74–75 They
cannavigate[379]to a den without having to retrace their outward route.[74][380]They
are not migratory.[28][381]: 45–46 
Octopuses bring captured prey to the den to eat. Dens are often surrounded by
amidden[382]of dead and uneaten food items. These middens may attract
scavengers such as fish, molluscs, andechinoderms[383].[75][384]On rare
occasions, octopuseshunt cooperatively with other species[385], with fish as
their partners. They regulate thespecies composition[386]of the hunting
group – and the behavior of their partners – by punching them.[76][387]
Feeding

[An octopus in an open seashell on a sandy surface, surrounding a small crab with the suckers on its arms][389]Veined
octopus[390]eating a crabOctopuses are generally predatory and feed on prey
such ascrustaceans[391],bivalves[392],gastropods[393], fish, and other
cephalopods, includingmembers of the same species[394].[77][395]: 47, 60 Major
items in the diet of the giant Pacific octopus include bivalves such as the
cockleClinocardium nuttallii[396], clams and scallops and crustaceans such ascrabs[397].
It typically rejectsmoon snails[398]because they are too large;limpets[399],rock
scallops[400],chitons[401]andabalone[402], because they are too securely fixed
to the rock.[75][403]Small cirrate octopuses such as those of the generaGrimpoteuthis[404]andOpisthoteuthis[405]typically
prey on polychaetes,copepods[406],amphipods[407]andisopods[408].[78][409]
Octopuses typically locate prey by feeling through their environment;[29][410]: 60 some
species hide and ambush their target.[77][411]: 54 When prey tries to
escape, the octopus jets after it.[29][412]: 61 Octopuses may drill into
the shells of crustaceans, bivalves and gastropods. It used to be thought that
drilling was done by the radula, but it has now been shown that minute teeth
at the tip of the salivary papilla are involved, and an enzyme in the toxic
saliva is used to dissolve the calcium carbonate of the shell. This can take
hours and once the shell is penetrated, the prey dies almost instantaneously.
With crabs, tough-shelled species are more likely to be drilled, and
soft-shelled crabs are torn apart.[79][413]
Some species have other modes of feeding.Grimpoteuthis[414]either lacks or has
a small radula and swallows prey whole.[38][415]In the deep-sea genusStauroteuthis[416],
the suckers in most species have been altered intophotophores[417]which are
believed to fool prey by directing them to the mouth, making them one of the
fewbioluminescent[418]octopuses.[80][419]
Locomotion

[An octopus swimming with its round body to the front, its arms forming a streamlined tube behind][421]Octopuses
swim with their arms trailing behind.Octopuses mainly move about by relatively
slow crawling with some swimming in a head-first position.Jet propulsion[422]or
backward swimming, is their fastest means of locomotion, while crawling is
slowest.[81][423]While crawling, the suckers adhere and detach from the
substrate as the animal hauls itself forward with its powerful arm muscles.[33][424][81][425]In
2005,Adopus aculeatus[426]and veined octopus (Amphioctopus marginatus[427])
were found to walk on two arms, while at the same time mimicking plant matter.[82][428]This
form of locomotion allows these octopuses to move quickly away from a
potential predator without being recognised.[81][429]Some species of octopus
can crawl out of the water briefly, which they may do between tide pools.[83][430][29][431]: 183 "Stilt
walking" is used by the veined octopus when carrying stacked coconut shells.
The octopus carries the shells underneath it with two arms, and progresses
with an awkward gait supported by its remaining arms, which are stiffened.[84][432]
[Three images in sequence of a two-finned sea creature swimming with an eight-cornered web][434]Movements
of the finned speciesCirroteuthis muelleri[435]Most octopuses swim by
expelling a jet of water from the mantle through the siphon into the sea. The
direction of travel depends on the orientation of the siphon. When swimming,
the head is at the front and the siphon is pointed backward but, when jetting,
the visceral hump leads, the siphon points at the head and the arms trail
behind, with the animal presenting afusiform[436]appearance. In an alternative
method of swimming, some species flatten themselves dorso-ventrally, and swim
with the arms splayed; this may provide lift and be faster than normal
swimming. Jetting is used to escape from danger, but is physiologically
inefficient, requiring a mantle pressure so high as to stop the heart from
beating, resulting in a progressive oxygen deficit.[81][437]
Cirrate octopuses cannot produce jet propulsion and swim using their fins.
Their neutrally buoyant bodies float along while the fins are spread. They can
also contract their arms and surrounding web to make sudden moves known as
"take-offs". Another form of locomotion is "pumping", which involves
symmetrical contractions of muscles in their webs producingperistaltic waves[438],
moving them slowly.[38][439]
Intelligence

Main article:Cephalopod intelligence[440][A captive octopus with two arms wrapped around the cap of a plastic container][442]Octopus
opening a container by unscrewing its capOctopuses are highlyintelligent[443].[85][444]Maze[445]andproblem-solving[446]experiments
have shown evidence of a memory system that can store bothshort-[447]andlong-term
memory[448].[86][449]In laboratory experiments, octopuses can readily be
trained to distinguish between different shapes and patterns. They have been
reported to practiseobservational learning[450],[87][451]although the validity
of these findings is contested.[85][452]Theveined octopus[453]collects
discardedcoconut[454]shells, then uses them to build a shelter, an example oftool
use[455].[84][456]Octopuses have also been observed in what has been described
asplay[457]: including moving around a bottle by jetting water at it.[88][458]Octopuses
often break out of aquariums and sometimes into others in search of food.[83][459][89][460][90][461]Interpretation
of evidence has been used to suggest that octopuses havesentience[462]and can
feelpain[463].[91][464]
Camouflage and colour change

Video ofOctopus cyaneamoving and changing its colour, shape, and textureOctopuses
usecamouflage[465]to hunt and to avoid predators. To do this, they use
specialised skin cells that change colour.Chromatophores[466]contain yellow,
orange, red, brown, or black pigments; most species have three of these
colours, while some have two or four. Other colour-changing cells are
reflective iridophores and white leucophores.[92][467]This colour-changing
ability is also used to communicate with or warn other octopuses.[29][468]: 90–97 The
energy cost of the complete activation of the chromatophore system is high,
nearly matching the energy used at rest.[93][469]
Octopuses can create distracting patterns with waves of dark colouration
across the body, a display known as the "passing cloud". Muscles in the skin
change the texture of the mantle to achieve greater camouflage. In some
species, the mantle can take on the bumpy appearance of algae-covered rocks.
Diurnal, shallow water octopuses have more complex skin than their nocturnal
and deep-sea counterparts. In the latter species, skin anatomy is limited to
one colour or pattern.[29][470]: 89–97 
Octopus' "moving rock" trick involves mimicking a rock and then inching across
the open space with a speed matching that of the surrounding water.[94][471]
Defence

[An octopus among coral displaying conspicuous rings of turquoise outlined in black against a sandy background][473]Warning
display[474]ofgreater blue-ringed octopus[475](Hapalochlaena lunulata)Aside
from humans, octopuses are prey for fishes,seabirds[476],sea otters[477],pinnipeds[478],cetaceans[479],
and other cephalopods.[95][480]Octopuses typically hide or disguise themselves
by camouflage andmimicry[481]; some have conspicuouswarning coloration
(aposematism)[482]ordeimatic behaviour[483]("bluffing" a threatening
appearance).[29][484]: 90–97 An octopus may hide in their dens for as
much as 40% of the day. When the octopus is approached, it may reach out an
arm to investigate. 66% ofE. dofleini[485]in one study had scars, with 50%
missing arms.[95][486]The blue rings of the venomous blue-ringed octopus are
hidden in muscular skin folds which contract when the animal is threatened,
revealing the iridescent warning.[96][487]TheAtlantic white-spotted octopus[488](Callistoctopus
macropus) becomes redder with bright white spots in adeimatic display[489].
Displays are often reinforced by stretching out the animal's arms, fins or web
to make it look as big and threatening as possible.[77][490]: 80–81 
Octopus try to escape from a predator by ejecting an ink cloud, which acts as
a "smoke-screen" or adecoy[491], as well as to interfere with the attacker's
sense of smell.[97][492]When severed by a predator, some octopuses candetach
their arm[493],[77][494]: 86–87 which cangrow back[495].[29][496]: 85 Some
octopuses, such as themimic octopus[497], can combine their flexible bodies
with their colour-changing ability to mimic other, more dangerous animals,
such aslionfish[498], sea snakes, andeels[499].[98][500][99][501]
Pathogens and parasites

Cephalopods are known to be the intermediate or finalhosts[502]of various
parasiticcestodes[503],nematodes[504]and copepods; 150 species ofprotistan[505]andmetazoan[506]parasites
are recognised.[100][507]TheDicyemidae[508]are a family of tiny worms found in
the renal appendages of many species;[101][509]it is unclear whether they are
parasitic orendosymbionts[510].Coccidians[511]in the genusAggregata[512]living
in the gut cause serious illness in the host. Octopuses have aninnate immune
system[513]; theirhaemocytes[514]locate the foreign invader and attack it viaphagocytosis[515],
encapsulation, infiltration, orcytotoxicity[516]. The haemocytes also
contribute to healing injures.[102][517]A gram-negative bacterium,Vibrio
lentus[518], can cause skin lesions, exposure of muscle and sometimes death.[103][519]
Evolution
---------

Further information:Evolution of cephalopods[520]The scientific name Octopoda
was first given as the order of octopuses in 1818 by English biologistWilliam
Elford Leach[521],[104][522]who classified them as Octopoida the previous
year.[2][523]Octopoda consists of around 300 known species[29][524]: 145 and
were historically divided into two suborders, theIncirrina[525]and the
Cirrina.[105][526]More recent evidence suggests cirrates are the most basal
species, not a uniqueclade[527].[106][528]The incirrate octopuses (the
majority of species) lack the cirri and paired swimming fins of the cirrates.[39][529]In
addition, the internal shell of incirrates is either present as a pair ofstylets[530]or
absent altogether.[107][531]
Fossil history and phylogeny

[Fossil of crown group coleoid on a slab of Jurassic rock from Germany][533]The
octopuses evolved from theMuensterelloidea[534](fossil pictured) in theJurassic[535]period.[108][536]The
Cephalopoda descended from a mollusc resembling theMonoplacophora[537]in theCambrian[538]some
530 million years ago. The Coleoidea, which brought their shells inside the
body, diverged from thenautiloids[539]in theDevonian[540]some 416 million
years ago. Around 276 million years ago, during thePermian[541], the coleoids
split into two groups, theVampyropoda[542]and theDecabrachia[543].[109][544]The
octopuses arose from theMuensterelloidea[545]within the Vampyropoda in theJurassic[546].
The earliest octopus likely lived near the sea floor (benthic[547]todemersal[548])
in shallow marine environments.[109][549][110][550][108][551]Octopuses consist
mostly of soft tissue, and so fossils are relatively rare. As soft-bodied
cephalopods, they lack the external shell of most molluscs, including other
cephalopods like the nautiloids and the (extinct)Ammonoidea[552].[111][553]They
have eight limbs like otherColeoidea[554], but lack the extra specialised
feeding appendages known astentacles[555]which are longer and thinner with
suckers only at their club-like ends.[112][556][113][557]The vampire squid (Vampyroteuthis[558])
also lacks tentacles but has sensory filaments.[114][559]

methodology.[115][565]Divergence dates are from Kröger et al., 2011 and Fuchs

RNA editing and the genome

Octopuses, like other coleoid cephalopods but unlike morebasal[618]cephalopods
or other molluscs, are capable of greaterRNA editing[619], changing thenucleic
acid sequence[620]of theprimary transcript[621]of RNA molecules, than any
other organisms. Much editing is done in the nervous system, particularly forexcitability[622]and
neuronal morphology. Coleoids rely mostly onADAR[623]enzymes for RNA editing,
which requires large,double-stranded RNA[624]structures. The many editing
sites areconserved[625]in the coleoid genome and the mutation rates for the
sites are hampered. Hence, greater transcriptome plasticity has come at the
cost of slower genome evolution.[116][626]
The genome of octopuses has also gone through several chromosomal fusions and
rearrangements, unlike that of their closest relative the vampire squid, whose
chromosomal structure is more basal and squid-like.[117][627]The octopus
genome is unremarkablybilaterian[628]except for large developments of two gene
families:protocadherins[629], which regulate the development of neurons; and
theC2H2 zinc-finger[630]transcription factors. Many novel genes in both
cephalopods generally and octopus specifically manifest in the animals' skin,
suckers, and nervous system.[45][631]

Cultural significance

[An ancient nearly spherical vase with 2 handles by the top, painted all over with an octopus decoration in black][633]Minoan[634]clay
vase with octopus decoration, c. 1500 BCAncient seafaring people were aware of
the octopus, as evidenced by artworks and designs. It was depicted on coins
during theMinoan civilization[635]possibly as early as 1650 BCE and on pottery
inMycenaean Greece[636]around between 1200 and 1100 BCE. A Hawaiiancreation
myth[637]suggests that the octopus is the lone survivor of a previous age. The
legendary sea monster, thekraken[638]is conceived as octopus-like.[28][639]: 1, 4–5 Similarly,Medusa[640]was
compared to an octopus, with her snake-hair resembling the creature's arms.[118][641]: 133 TheAkkorokamui[642]is
a gigantic octopus-likemonster[643]fromAinu[644]folklore, worshipped inShinto[645].[119][646]
In theAsuka-era[647]Japanese legendTaishokan, a female diver battles an
octopus to recover a stolen jewel, which became the inspiration forwoodblock
printings[648]. Similarly, in the 1973 novelGravity's Rainbow[649]an octopus
named Grigori attacks a woman on the beach. A battle with an octopus plays a
significant role inVictor Hugo[650]'s 1866 bookTravailleurs de la mer(Toilers
of the Sea[651]). The octopus continues to be depicted as antagonistic in
films such asWake of the Red Witch[652](1948).[118][653]: 129–131, 138–139, 145–147 
Inpolitical cartoons[654], octopuses have been used to symbolise empires and
large organizations, the arms representing long reach. Octopuses also have an
erotic appeal. Japanese erotic art,shunga[655], includesukiyo-e[656]woodblock
prints such asKatsushika Hokusai[657]'s 1814 printTako to ama(The Dream of the
Fisherman's Wife[658]), in which a woman is sexually intertwined with a large
and a small octopus. This art style would inspirePablo Picasso[659]'s 1903
drawingAn Erotic Drawing: Woman and Octopus.[118][660]: 126–128 Some
individual octopuses gained celebrity status, notablyPaul the Octopus[661]who
predicted the winners of the2010 FIFA World Cup[662].[28][663]: 3–4 
Danger to humans

Further information:Cephalopod attack[664][Coloured drawing of a huge octopus rising from the sea and attacking a sailing ship's three masts with its spiralling arms][666]Pen
andwash[667]drawing of an imaginedcolossal octopus[668]attacking a ship, by
themalacologist[669]Pierre de Montfort[670], 1801Octopuses generally avoid
humans, but some conflictual incidents have been verified. For example, a
2.4-metre (8 ft) Pacific octopus, said to be nearly perfectly camouflaged,
"lunged" at a diver and "wrangled" over his camera before it let go. Another
diver recorded the encounter on video.[120][671]All species are venomous, but
only blue-ringed octopuses have venom that is lethal to humans.[121][672]Blue-ringed
octopuses rank amongst the most dangerous marine animals; their bites are
reported each year across the animals' range from Australia to the eastern
Indo-Pacific Ocean. They bite only when provoked or accidentally touched;
bites are small and usually painless. The venom appears to be able to
penetrate the skin without a puncture, given prolonged contact. It containstetrodotoxin[673],
which causes paralysis by blocking the transmission ofnerve impulses[674]to
the muscles. This causes death by respiratory failure leading tocerebral
anoxia[675]. No antidote is known, but if breathing can be kept going
artificially, patients recover within 24 hours.[122][676][123][677]Bites have
been recorded from captive octopuses of other species; they leave temporary
swellings.[30][678]: 68 
As a food source

Main article:Octopus as food[679][image][681]Octopussushi[682]Octopusesare
fished[683]around the world and between 1988 and 1995, catches varied between
245,320 and 322,999 metric tons.[124][684]The world catch peaked in 2007 at
380,000 tons, and had fallen by a tenth by 2012.[125][685]Methods to capture
octopuses include pots,trapping[686],trawling[687], snaring, drift fishing,
spearing, hooking and catching by hands.[124][688]Octopuses are alsobycatch[689].[126][690]Attempts
to farm octopuses commercially are controversial.[127][691][128][692]
Octopus is eaten in many cultures, such as those on the Mediterranean and
Asian coasts.[129][693]The arms and other body parts are prepared in ways that
vary by species and geography. Live octopuses or their wriggling pieces are
consumed assan-nakji[694]in Korean cuisine.[130][695][131][696]If not prepared
properly, however, the severed arms can choke the diner with their suction
cups, causing at least one death in 2010.[132][697]Animal welfare groups have
objected to the live consumption of octopuses on the basis that they can
experience pain.[133][698]
Science and technology

In classical Greece,Aristotle[699](384–322 BC)commented on[700]their
colour-changing abilities, both for camouflage and forsignalling[701], in hisHistoria
animalium[702]: "The octopus ... seeks its prey by so changing its colour as
to render it like the colour of the stones adjacent to it; it does so alsowhen
alarmed[703]."[134][704]Aristotle noted that the octopus had a hectocotyl arm
and suggested it might be used in reproduction. This claim was widely ignored
until the 19th century. It was described in 1829 by the French zoologistGeorges
Cuvier[705], who supposed it to be a parasitic worm, naming it as a new
species,Hectocotylus octopodis.[135][706][136][707][137][708]Other zoologists
thought it a spermatophore; the German zoologistHeinrich Müller[709]believed
it was "designed" to detach during copulation. In 1856, the Danish zoologistJapetus
Steenstrup[710]demonstrated that it is used to transfer sperm, and only rarely
detaches.[138][711]
[image][713]Flexiblebiomimetic[714]'Octopus'robotics[715]arm. The BioRobotics
Institute,Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna[716],Pisa[717], 2011[139][718]Octopuses
offer manypossibilities in biological research[719]; the California two-spot
octopus had its genome sequenced, allowing exploration of its molecular
adaptations.[45][720]Havingindependently evolved[721]mammal-like intelligence,
octopuses were compared by the philosopherPeter Godfrey-Smith[722], who
studied the nature of intelligence,[140][723]to hypotheticalintelligent
extraterrestrials[724].[141][725]Their intelligence and flexible bodies enable
them to escape from supposedly secure tanks inpublic aquariums[726].[142][727]
Due to their intelligence, many argue that octopuses should be given
protections when used for experiments.[143][728]In the UK from 1993 to 2012,
the common octopus (Octopus vulgaris) was the only invertebrate protected
under theAnimals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986[729].[144][730]In 2012, this
legislation was extended to include all cephalopods[145][731]in accordance
with a generalEU[732]directive.[146][733]
Somerobotics[734]research is exploringbiomimicry[735]of octopus features.
Octopus arms can move and sense largely autonomously without intervention from
the animal's central nervous system. In 2015 a team in Italy built soft-bodied
robots able to crawl and swim, requiring only minimal computation.[147][736][148][737]In
2017, a German company made an arm with a softpneumatically[738]controlledsilicone[739]gripper
fitted with two rows of suckers. It was able to grasp objects such as a metal
tube, a magazine, or a ball, and to fill a glass by pouring water from a
bottle.[149][740]
