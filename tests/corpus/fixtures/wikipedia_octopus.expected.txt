An octopus (pl.: octopuses or octopodes[a][31]) is a soft-bodied, eight-limbed
mollusc[32] of the order[33] Octopoda (/ɒkˈtɒpədə/[34], ok-TOP-ə-də[35]
[3][36]). The order consists of some 300 species[37] and is grouped within the
class Cephalopoda[38] with squids[39], cuttlefish[40], and nautiloids[41].
Like other cephalopods, an octopus is bilaterally symmetric[42] with two eyes
and a beaked[43] mouth at the centre point of the eight limbs.[b][44] An
octopus can radically deform its shape, enabling it to squeeze through small
gaps. They trail their appendages behind them as they swim backwards. The
siphon[45] is used for respiration[46] and locomotion[47] (by water jet
propulsion[48]). Octopuses have a complex nervous system and excellent sight,
and are among the most intelligent and behaviourally diverse invertebrates[49]
.
Octopuses inhabit various ocean[50] habitats, including coral reefs[51],
pelagic[52] waters, and the seabed[53]; some live in the intertidal zone[54]
and others at abyssal depths[55]. Most species grow quickly, mature early, and
are short-lived. In most species, the male uses a specially-adapted arm to
deliver sperm directly into the female's mantle cavity, after which he becomes
senescent[56] and dies, while the female deposits fertilised eggs in a den and
cares for them until they hatch, after which she also dies. They are predators
and hunt crustaceans[57], bivalves[58], gastropods[59] and fish. Strategies to
defend themselves against their own predators include expelling ink[60],
camouflage[61], and threat displays[62], the ability to jet quickly through
the water and hide, and deceit. All octopuses are venomous[63], but only the
blue-ringed octopuses[64] are known to be deadly to humans.
Octopuses appear in mythology[65] as sea monsters such as the kraken[66] of
Norway and the Akkorokamui[67] of the Ainu[68], and possibly the Gorgons[69]
of ancient Greece[70]. A battle with an octopus appears in Victor Hugo[71]'s
book Toilers of the Sea[72]. Octopuses appear in Japanese shunga[73] erotic
art. They are eaten and considered a delicacy by humans in many parts of the
world, especially the Mediterranean[74] and Asia.
Etymology and pluralisation
---------------------------

The scientific Latin[75] term octopus[76] was derived from Ancient Greek[77]
ὀκτώπους[78] (oktōpous), a compound[79] form of ὀκτώ[80] (oktō
, 'eight') and πούς[81] (pous, 'foot'), itself a variant form of
ὀκτάπους[82], a word used for example by Alexander of Tralles[83] (c.
 525 – c. 605).[5][84][6][85][7][86]
The standard pluralised[87] form of octopus in English is octopuses;[8][88]
the Ancient Greek plural ὀκτώποδες, octopodes (/ɒkˈtɒpədiːz
/[89]), has also been used historically.[9][90] The alternative plural octopi
is usually considered etymologically incorrect, because it wrongly assumes[91]
that octopus is a Latin second-declension[92] -us[93] noun or adjective when,
in either Greek or Latin, it is a third-declension[94] noun.[10][95][11][96]
Historically, the first plural to commonly appear in English language sources,
in the early 19th century, is the Latinate form octopi,[12][97] followed by
the English form octopuses in the latter half of the same century. The
Hellenic plural is roughly contemporary in usage, although it is also the
rarest.[13][98]
A Dictionary of Modern English Usage[99] by H. W. Fowler states that the only
acceptable plural in English is octopuses, that octopi is misconceived, and
octopodes pedantic[100];[14][101][15][102][16][103] the last is nonetheless
used frequently enough to be acknowledged by the descriptivist[104]
Merriam-Webster 11th Collegiate Dictionary and Webster's New World College
Dictionary.[17][105] The Oxford English Dictionary[106] lists octopuses,
octopi, and octopodes, in that order, reflecting frequency of use, calling
octopodes rare and noting that octopi is based on a misunderstanding.[18][107]
The New Oxford American Dictionary[108] (3rd Edition, 2010) lists octopuses as
the only acceptable pluralisation, and indicates that octopodes is
occasionally used, but that octopi is incorrect.[19][109]
Anatomy and physiology
----------------------

Size

[Captured specimen of a giant octopus][111] A giant Pacific octopus[112] at
Echizen Matsushima Aquarium, Japan The giant Pacific octopus[113] (
Enteroctopus dofleini) is often cited as the largest octopus species. Adults
usually weigh 10–50 kg (22–110 lb), with an arm span of up to 4.8 m
(16 ft).[20][114] The largest specimen of this species to be scientifically
documented reached a live mass of 71 kg (157 lb).[21][115] Much larger sizes
have been claimed:[22][116] one specimen was recorded as 600 lb (270 kg)
with an arm span of 32 ft (9.8 m).[23][117] A carcass of the seven-arm
octopus[118], Haliphron atlanticus[119], weighed 61 kg (134 lb) and was
estimated to have had a live mass of 75 kg (165 lb).[24][120][25][121] The
smallest species is Octopus wolfi[122], which is around 2.5 cm (1 in) and
weighs less than 1 g (0.035 oz).[26][123]
External characteristics

The octopus has an elongated body that is bilaterally symmetrical[124] along
its dorso-ventral (back to belly) axis; the head and foot[125] are on the
ventral side, but act as the anterior (front). The head contains both the
mouth and the brain.[27][126]: 343–344  The mouth has a sharp
chitinous[127] beak[128] and is surrounded by and underneath the foot, which
evolved into flexible, prehensile limbs[129], known as "arms", which are
attached to each other near their base by a webbed structure.[27][130]
: 343–344 [28][131]: 40–41 [29][132]: 13–15  The arms can be
described based on side and sequence position (such as L1, R1, L2, R2) and
divide into four pairs.[30][133]: 12  The two rear appendages are
generally used to walk on the sea floor, while the other six are used to
forage for food.[31][134] The bulbous and hollow mantle[135] is fused to the
back of the head and contains most of the vital organs.[29][136]: 13–15 
[28][137]: 40–41  The mantle also has a cavity with muscular walls and a
pair of gills; it is connected to the exterior by a funnel or siphon[138].
[27][139]: 343–344 [32][140]
[Schematic of external anatomy][142] Diagram of octopus from side, with gills,
funnel, eye, ocellus[143] (eyespot), web, arms, suckers, hectocotylus[144] and
ligula[145] labelled. The skin consists of a thin epidermis[146] with mucous
cells and sensory cells and a fibrous dermis[147] made of collagen[148] and
containing various cells that allow colour change.[27][149]: 362  Most of
the body is made of soft tissue, allowing it to squeeze through tiny gaps;
even the larger species can pass through a gap little more than 2.5 cm
(1 in) in diameter.[28][150]: 40–41  Lacking skeletal support, the arms
work as muscular hydrostats[151] and feature longitudinal, transverse, and
circular muscles around a central axial nerve. They can squash and stretch,
coil at any place in any direction or stiffen.[33][152][34][153]
The interior surfaces of the arms are covered with circular, adhesive suckers.
The suckers allow the octopus to secure itself in place or to handle objects.
Each sucker is typically circular and bowl-like and has two distinct parts: an
outer disc-shaped infundibulum[154] and an inner cup-like acetabulum[155],
both of which are thick muscles covered in connective tissue[156]. A
chitinous[157] cuticle lines the outer surface. When a sucker attaches to a
surface, the orifice between the two structures is sealed and the infundibulum
flattens. Muscle contractions allow for attachment and detachment.[35][158]
[36][159][33][160] Each of the eight arms senses and responds to light,
allowing the octopus to control its limbs even if its head is obscured.[
37][161]
[A stubby round sea-creature with short ear-like fins][163] A finned
Grimpoteuthis[164] species with its atypical octopus body plan The cranium has
two cartilaginous[165] capsules each containing one large eye, which resembles
those of fish. The cornea[166] is formed from a translucent[167] epidermal
layer; the slit-shaped pupil[168] forms a hole in the iris[169] just behind
the cornea. The lens hangs behind the pupil; photoreceptive retinal cells[170]
line the back. The pupil can expand and contract; a retinal pigment screens
incident light in bright conditions.[27][171]: 360–361 
Some species differ in form from the typical body shape. Basal[172] species,
the Cirrina[173], have gelatinous bodies with two fins[174] located above the
eyes, an internal shell[175] and mostly webbed arms that are lined with fleshy
papillae or cirri[176] underneath.[38][177][39][178]
Circulatory system

Octopuses have a closed circulatory system[179], in which the blood remains
inside blood vessels. They have three hearts; a systemic or main heart that
circulates blood around the body and two branchial or gill hearts that pump it
through the two gills. The systemic heart becomes inactive when the animal is
swimming. Thus, the octopus loses energy quickly and mostly crawls.[30][180]
: 19–20, 31–35 [28][181]: 42–43  Octopus blood contains the
copper[182]-rich protein haemocyanin[183] to transport oxygen. This makes the
blood viscous[184] and it requires great pressure to pump it around the body;
blood pressures[185] can surpass 75 mmHg (10 kPa).[30][186]: 31–35 
[28][187]: 42–43 [40][188] In cold conditions with low oxygen levels,
haemocyanin transports oxygen more efficiently than haemoglobin[189].[41][190]
The haemocyanin is dissolved in the blood plasma[191] instead of carried
within blood cells and gives the blood a bluish colour.[30][192]: 31–35 
[28][193]: 42–43 [29][194]: 22 
The systemic heart has muscular contractile walls and consists of a single
ventricle[195] and two atria[196], which attach it to each of the two gills.
The blood vessels consist of arteries, capillaries and veins and are lined
with a cellular endothelium[197] unlike that of most other invertebrates[198].
The blood circulates through the aorta[199] and capillary system, to the
venae cavae[200], after which the blood is pumped through the gills by the
branchial hearts and back to the main heart. Much of the venous system is
contractile, which helps circulate the blood.[27][201]: 358 
Respiration

[An octopus on the seabed, its siphon protruding near its eye][203] Octopus
with open siphon. The siphon is used for respiration, waste disposal and
discharging ink. Respiration[204] involves drawing water into the mantle
cavity through an aperture, passing it through the gills, and expelling it
through the siphon. Ingress is achieved by contraction of radial muscles in
the mantle wall, and flapper valves shut when strong, circular muscles expel
the water through the siphon.[42][205] Extensive connective tissue lattices
support the respiratory muscles and allow them to inflate the respiratory
chamber.[30][206]: 24–26  The lamella[207] structure of the gills allows
for high oxygen uptake, up to 65% in water at 20 °C (68 °F).[43][208]
Respiration can also play a role in locomotion, as an octopus can propel its
body shooting water out of the siphon.[30][209]: 18 [40][210]
The thin skin absorbs additional oxygen. When resting, around 41% of oxygen
absorption is through the skin, reduced to 33% when the octopus swims, despite
the amount of oxygen absorption increasing as water flows over the body. When
it is resting after a meal, skin absorption can drop to 3%.[44][211]
Digestion and excretion

The digestive system begins with the buccal mass[212] which consists of the
mouth with the beak, the pharynx[213], radula[214] and salivary glands.[
30][215]: 71–74  The radula is serrated and made of chitin[216].[28
][217]: 40–41  Food is broken down and is forced into the esophagus[218]
by two lateral extensions of the esophageal side walls in addition to the
radula. From there it is transferred to the gastrointestinal tract[219], which
is mostly suspended from the roof of the mantle cavity. The tract consists of
a crop[220], where the food is stored; a stomach, where it is mixed with other
gut material; a caecum[221] where the food is separated into particles and
liquids and which absorbs fats; the digestive gland[222], where liver cells
break down and absorb the fluid and become "brown bodies"; and the intestine,
where the built-up waste is turned into faecal ropes by secretions and ejected
out of the funnel via the rectum[223].[30][224]: 75–79 
During osmoregulation[225], fluid is added to the pericardia[226] of the
branchial hearts. The octopus has two nephridia[227] (equivalent to vertebrate
kidneys) that are associated with the branchial hearts; these and their
associated ducts connect the pericardial cavities with the mantle cavity. Each
branch of the vena cava[228] has renal appendages that pass over the
thin-walled nephridium before reaching the branchial heart. Urine is created
in the pericardial cavity, and is altered by excretion, of mostly ammonia, and
absorption from the renal appendages, as it is passed along the associated
duct and through the nephridiopore into the mantle cavity.[27][229]
: 358–359 
A common octopus[230] (Octopus vulgaris) moving around. Its nervous system
allows the arms to move with some autonomy.Nervous system and senses

Octopuses and their relatives have a more expansive and complex nervous
system[231] than other invertebrates, containing over 500 million neurons[232]
, around the same as a dog.[45][233][46][234][47][235] One part is localised
in the brain, contained in a cartilaginous capsule. Two-thirds of the neurons
are in the nerve cords of its arms. This allows their arms to perform actions
with a degree of independence.[48][236] Learning mainly occurs in the brain,
while arms make decisions independently when supplied with information.[
49][237] A severed arm can still move and respond to stimuli.[50][238] Unlike
in many other animals, including other mollusks, the movement of octopuses and
their relatives are not organised in their brains via internal
somatotopic maps[239] of their bodies.[51][240] Octopuses have the same
jumping genes[241] that are active in the human brain, implying an
evolutionary convergence[242] at molecular level.[52][243]
[Close up of an octopus showing its eye and an arm with suckers][245] Eye of
common octopus[246] Like other cephalopods, octopuses have camera-like eyes.
[45][247] Colour vision[248] appears to vary from species to species, for
example, it is present in A. aegina[249] but absent in O. vulgaris[250].[
53][251] Opsins[252] in the skin respond to different wavelengths of light and
help the animals choose a colouration that matches the surroundings and
camouflages them; chromatophores[253] in the skin can respond to light
independently of the eyes.[54][254][55][255] An alternative hypothesis is that
cephalopod eyes[256] in species that only have a single photoreceptor
protein[257] may use chromatic aberration[258] to turn monochromatic vision
into colour vision, though this lowers image quality. This would explain
pupils shaped like the letter "U", the letter "W", or a dumbbell[259], as well
as the need for colourful mating displays.[56][260]
Attached to the optic capsules are two organs called statocysts[261] (sac-like
structures containing a mineralised mass and sensitive hairs), that allow the
octopus to sense the orientation of its body, relative to both gravity and
time (angular acceleration[262]). An autonomic[263] response keeps the
octopus's eyes oriented so that the pupil is always horizontal.[27][264]
: 360–361  Octopuses may also use the statocyst to hear. The common
octopus can hear sounds between 400 Hz and 1000 Hz, and hears best at
600 Hz.[57][265]
Octopuses have an excellent somatosensory system[266]. Their suction cups are
equipped with chemoreceptors[267] so they can taste[268] what they touch.
[58][269] Octopus arms move easily because the sensors recognise octopus skin
and prevent self-attachment.[59][270] Octopuses appear to have poor
proprioceptive[271] sense and must see their arms to keep track of their
position.[60][272][61][273]
Ink sac

The ink sac[274] is located under the digestive gland. A gland attached to the
sac produces the ink[275], and the sac holds it. The sac is close enough to
the funnel for the octopus to shoot out the ink with a water jet. As the
animal begins to shoot, the ink passes through glands which mix it with mucus
and it leaves the funnel as a thick, dark blob, which helps the animal to
escape from a predator.[29][276]: 107  The main pigment[277] in the ink is
melanin[278], which gives it its black colour.[62][279] Cirrate octopuses
usually lack the ink sac.[38][280]
Life cycle
----------

Reproduction

[Drawing of a male octopus with one large arm ending in the sexual apparatus][282]
Adult male Tremoctopus violaceus[283] with hectocotylus[284] Octopuses
have two sexes[285] and have only one gonad (testis[286] in males and
ovary[287] in females) located posteriorly. The gonad deposits gametes[288]
into an adjacent cavity called the gonocoel. A gonoduct[289] bridges the
gonocoel with the mantle cavity[290].[27][291]: 363–365  An optic
gland[292] creates hormones that cause the octopus to mature and age and
stimulate gamete production. The timing of reproduction and lifespan depends
on environmental conditions such as temperature, light and nutrition, which
trigger the gland.[29][293]: 147 [63][294] The male has a specialised arm
called a hectocotylus[295] which it uses to transfer spermatophores[296]
(packets of sperm) into the female's mantle cavity.[27][297]: 363–365 
The hectocotylus in Octopus is usually the R3 arm, which has a spoon-shaped
depression and a suckerless tip.[30][298]: 12–14 [27][299]
: 363–365  Fertilisation may occur in the mantle cavity or in the
surrounding water.[27][300]: 363–365 
Reproduction has been studied in some species. In the giant Pacific
octopus[301], courtship includes changes in skin texture and colour, mostly in
the male. The male may cling to the top or side of the female or position
himself beside her. There is some speculation that he may first use his
hectocotylus to remove any spermatophore or sperm already present in the
female. He picks up a spermatophore from his spermatophoric sac with the
hectocotylus, inserts it into the female's mantle cavity, and deposits it in
the correct location in the opening of the oviduct. Two spermatophores are
transferred in this way; these are about one metre (yard) long, and the empty
ends may protrude from the female's mantle.[64][302] A complex hydraulic
mechanism releases the sperm from the spermatophore.[27][303]: 363–365 
[A female octopus underneath hanging strings of her eggs][305] Female giant
Pacific octopus guarding strings of eggs The eggs have large yolks;
cleavage[306] (division) is relatively shallow and a germinal disc[307]
develops at the pole. During gastrulation[308], the disc surrounds the yolk,
forming a yolk sac, which eventually forms part of the gut. The embryo forms
as the dorsal side of the disc grows upward, with a shell gland, gills, mantle
and eyes on its dorsal side. The arms and funnel form on the ventral side of
the disc, with the former moving upward to surround the mouth. The embryo
consumes the yolk during development.[27][309]: 363–365 
Over a month after mating, giant Pacific octopuses lay eggs. The species can
lay 180,000 eggs in a single clutch, while O. rubescens[310] clutches host up
to 45,000 eggs while O. vulgaris clutches can include 500,000 eggs.[65][311]
: 75  Fertilised octopus eggs are laid as strings within a shelter.[
64][312][29][313]: 26  Female giant Pacific octopuses nurture and protect
their eggs for five months (160 days) until they hatch.[64][314] In colder
waters, such as those off Alaska[315], it may take up to ten months for the
eggs to completely develop.[65][316]: 74  In the argonaut[317] (paper
nautilus), the female is much larger than the male. She secretes a thin shell
shaped like a cornucopia[318], in which the eggs are deposited and in which
she also resides and broods the young while swimming.[29][319]: 26, 141 
[A microscopic view of a small round-bodied transparent animal with very short arms][321]
Octopus paralarva[322], a planktonic[323] hatchling Most young octopuses hatch
as paralarvae[324],[27][325]: 363–365  Octopus larvae in particular are
planktonic[326] for weeks or months. Larvae feed on shrimp, isopods and
amphipods[327], eventually settling on the ocean floor to mature.[30][328]
: 178  Species that produce larger eggs instead hatch as benthic animals
similar to the adults.[65][329]: 74–75  These include the southern
blue-ringed[330], Caribbean reef[331], California two-spot[332] and
Eledone moschata[333].[66][334]
Lifespan

Octopuses have short lifespans, living up to four years.[29][335]: 17  The
lifecycles of some species finish in less than half a year.[28][336]: 152 
For most octopuses, the ultimate life stage is senescence[337]. It is the
breakdown of cellular function without repair or replacement. It may last from
weeks to a few months at most. Males senesce after maturity, while for
females, it comes after they lay an egg clutch. During senescence, an octopus
does not feed, quickly weakens, and becomes sluggish. Lesions begin to form
and the octopus literally degenerates. They may die of starvation or get
picked off by predators.[67][338] Senescence is triggered by the optic glands
and experimental removal of them after spawning was found to extend their
lifecycle and activity.[68][339]
Distribution and habitat
------------------------

[An octopus nearly hidden in a crack in some coral][341] Octopus cyanea in
Kona, Hawaii Octopuses inhabit every ocean, with species adapted to many
habitats[342]. As juveniles, common octopuses inhabit shallow tide pools[343].
The Hawaiian day octopus (Octopus cyanea[344]) lives on coral reefs, while
argonauts[345] float in pelagic waters[346]. Abdopus aculeatus[347] is a
near-shore species and can be found in seagrass[348] beds. Some species can
survive in deeper environments. The spoon-armed octopus (Bathypolypus
arcticus[349]) can live 1,000 m (3,300 ft) deep, and Vulcanoctopus
hydrothermalis[350] lives in depths of 2,000 m (6,600 ft) around
hydrothermal vents[351].[29][352]: 13–15  Species such as
Megaleledone setebos[353] and Pareledone charcoti[354], can survive in the
waters of the Antarctic, which reach −1.8 °C (29 °F).[41][355] No
species are known to live in fresh water.[69][356]
The cirrate[357] species are often free-swimming and live in deep-water
habitats.[39][358] Although several species live at bathyal[359] and
abyssal[360] depths, only a single indisputable record documents their
presence in the hadal zone[361]; a species of Grimpoteuthis[362] (dumbo
octopus) photographed at 6,957 m (22,825 ft).[70][363]
Behaviour and ecology
---------------------

Octopuses are mostly solitary[29][364]: 17, 134  though a few are known
to live in groups and interact regularly, usually in the context of
dominance[365] and reproductive competition. This is likely the result of
abundant food supplies combined with fewer den sites.[71][366] The Larger
Pacific striped octopus[367] has been described as particularly social, living
in groups of up to 40.[72][368][73][369] Octopuses hide in dens, which are
typically crevices in rocky or other hard structures, including man-made ones.
Small species may use abandoned shells and bottles.[29][370]
: 69, 74–75  They can navigate[371] to a den without having to retrace
their outward route.[74][372] They are not migratory.[28][373]: 45–46 
Octopuses bring captured prey to the den to eat. Dens are often surrounded by
a midden[374] of dead and uneaten food items. These middens may attract
scavengers such as fish, molluscs, and echinoderms[375].[75][376] On rare
occasions, octopuses hunt cooperatively with other species[377], with fish as
their partners. They regulate the species composition[378] of the hunting
group – and the behavior of their partners – by punching them.[76][379]
Feeding

[An octopus in an open seashell on a sandy surface, surrounding a small crab with the suckers on its arms][381]
Veined octopus[382] eating a crab Octopuses are generally predatory and feed
on prey such as crustaceans[383], bivalves[384], gastropods[385], fish, and
other cephalopods, including members of the same species[386].[77][387]
: 47, 60  Major items in the diet of the giant Pacific octopus include
bivalves such as the cockle Clinocardium nuttallii[388], clams and scallops
and crustaceans such as crabs[389]. It typically rejects moon snails[390]
because they are too large; limpets[391], rock scallops[392], chitons[393] and
abalone[394], because they are too securely fixed to the rock.[75][395] Small
cirrate octopuses such as those of the genera Grimpoteuthis[396] and
Opisthoteuthis[397] typically prey on polychaetes, copepods[398],
amphipods[399] and isopods[400].[78][401]
Octopuses typically locate prey by feeling through their environment;[29][402]
: 60  some species hide and ambush their target.[77][403]: 54  When
prey tries to escape, the octopus jets after it.[29][404]: 61  Octopuses
may drill into the shells of crustaceans, bivalves and gastropods. It used to
be thought that drilling was done by the radula, but it has now been shown
that minute teeth at the tip of the salivary papilla are involved, and an
enzyme in the toxic saliva is used to dissolve the calcium carbonate of the
shell. This can take hours and once the shell is penetrated, the prey dies
almost instantaneously. With crabs, tough-shelled species are more likely to
be drilled, and soft-shelled crabs are torn apart.[79][405]
Some species have other modes of feeding. Grimpoteuthis[406] either lacks or
has a small radula and swallows prey whole.[38][407] In the deep-sea genus
Stauroteuthis[408], the suckers in most species have been altered into
photophores[409] which are believed to fool prey by directing them to the
mouth, making them one of the few bioluminescent[410] octopuses.[80][411]
Locomotion

[An octopus swimming with its round body to the front, its arms forming a streamlined tube behind][413]
Octopuses swim with their arms trailing behind. Octopuses mainly move about by
relatively slow crawling with some swimming in a head-first position. Jet
propulsion[414] or backward swimming, is their fastest means of locomotion,
while crawling is slowest.[81][415] While crawling, the suckers adhere and
detach from the substrate as the animal hauls itself forward with its powerful
arm muscles.[33][416][81][417] In 2005, Adopus aculeatus[418] and veined
octopus (Amphioctopus marginatus[419]) were found to walk on two arms, while
at the same time mimicking plant matter.[82][420] This form of locomotion
allows these octopuses to move quickly away from a potential predator without
being recognised.[81][421] Some species of octopus can crawl out of the water
briefly, which they may do between tide pools.[83][422][29][423]: 183 
"Stilt walking" is used by the veined octopus when carrying stacked coconut
shells. The octopus carries the shells underneath it with two arms, and
progresses with an awkward gait supported by its remaining arms, which are
stiffened.[84][424]
[Three images in sequence of a two-finned sea creature swimming with an eight-cornered web][426]
Movements of the finned species Cirroteuthis muelleri[427] Most octopuses swim
by expelling a jet of water from the mantle through the siphon into the sea.
The direction of travel depends on the orientation of the siphon. When
swimming, the head is at the front and the siphon is pointed backward but,
when jetting, the visceral hump leads, the siphon points at the head and the
arms trail behind, with the animal presenting a fusiform[428] appearance. In
an alternative method of swimming, some species flatten themselves
dorso-ventrally, and swim with the arms splayed; this may provide lift and be
faster than normal swimming. Jetting is used to escape from danger, but is
physiologically inefficient, requiring a mantle pressure so high as to stop
the heart from beating, resulting in a progressive oxygen deficit.[81][429]
Cirrate octopuses cannot produce jet propulsion and swim using their fins.
Their neutrally buoyant bodies float along while the fins are spread. They can
also contract their arms and surrounding web to make sudden moves known as
"take-offs". Another form of locomotion is "pumping", which involves
symmetrical contractions of muscles in their webs producing peristaltic
waves[430], moving them slowly.[38][431]
Intelligence

[A captive octopus with two arms wrapped around the cap of a plastic container][433]
Octopus opening a container by unscrewing its cap Octopuses are highly
intelligent[434].[85][435] Maze[436] and problem-solving[437] experiments have
shown evidence of a memory system that can store both short-[438] and
long-term memory[439].[86][440] In laboratory experiments, octopuses can
readily be trained to distinguish between different shapes and patterns. They
have been reported to practise observational learning[441],[87][442] although
the validity of these findings is contested.[85][443] The veined octopus[444]
collects discarded coconut[445] shells, then uses them to build a shelter, an
example of tool use[446].[84][447] Octopuses have also been observed in what
has been described as play[448]: including moving around a bottle by jetting
water at it.[88][449] Octopuses often break out of aquariums and sometimes
into others in search of food.[83][450][89][451][90][452] Interpretation of
evidence has been used to suggest that octopuses have sentience[453] and can
feel pain[454].[91][455]
Camouflage and colour change

Video of Octopus cyanea moving and changing its colour, shape, and texture
Octopuses use camouflage[456] to hunt and to avoid predators. To do this, they
use specialised skin cells that change colour. Chromatophores[457] contain
yellow, orange, red, brown, or black pigments; most species have three of
these colours, while some have two or four. Other colour-changing cells are
reflective iridophores and white leucophores.[92][458] This colour-changing
ability is also used to communicate with or warn other octopuses.[29][459]
: 90–97  The energy cost of the complete activation of the chromatophore
system is high, nearly matching the energy used at rest.[93][460]
Octopuses can create distracting patterns with waves of dark colouration
across the body, a display known as the "passing cloud". Muscles in the skin
change the texture of the mantle to achieve greater camouflage. In some
species, the mantle can take on the bumpy appearance of algae-covered rocks.
Diurnal, shallow water octopuses have more complex skin than their nocturnal
and deep-sea counterparts. In the latter species, skin anatomy is limited to
one colour or pattern.[29][461]: 89–97 
Octopus' "moving rock" trick involves mimicking a rock and then inching across
the open space with a speed matching that of the surrounding water.[94][462]
Defence

[An octopus among coral displaying conspicuous rings of turquoise outlined in black against a sandy background][464]
Warning display[465] of greater blue-ringed octopus[466] (Hapalochlaena
lunulata) Aside from humans, octopuses are prey for fishes, seabirds[467],
sea otters[468], pinnipeds[469], cetaceans[470], and other cephalopods.[
95][471] Octopuses typically hide or disguise themselves by camouflage and
mimicry[472]; some have conspicuous warning coloration (aposematism)[473] or
deimatic behaviour[474] ("bluffing" a threatening appearance).[29][475]
: 90–97  An octopus may hide in their dens for as much as 40% of the
day. When the octopus is approached, it may reach out an arm to investigate.
66% of E. dofleini[476] in one study had scars, with 50% missing arms.[95
][477] The blue rings of the venomous blue-ringed octopus are hidden in
muscular skin folds which contract when the animal is threatened, revealing
the iridescent warning.[96][478] The Atlantic white-spotted octopus[479] (
Callistoctopus macropus) becomes redder with bright white spots in a
deimatic display[480]. Displays are often reinforced by stretching out the
animal's arms, fins or web to make it look as big and threatening as possible.
[77][481]: 80–81 
Octopus try to escape from a predator by ejecting an ink cloud, which acts as
a "smoke-screen" or a decoy[482], as well as to interfere with the attacker's
sense of smell.[97][483] When severed by a predator, some octopuses can
detach their arm[484],[77][485]: 86–87  which can grow back[486].[
29][487]: 85  Some octopuses, such as the mimic octopus[488], can combine
their flexible bodies with their colour-changing ability to mimic other, more
dangerous animals, such as lionfish[489], sea snakes, and eels[490].[98][491]
[99][492]
Pathogens and parasites

Cephalopods are known to be the intermediate or final hosts[493] of various
parasitic cestodes[494], nematodes[495] and copepods; 150 species of
protistan[496] and metazoan[497] parasites are recognised.[100][498] The
Dicyemidae[499] are a family of tiny worms found in the renal appendages of
many species;[101][500] it is unclear whether they are parasitic or
endosymbionts[501]. Coccidians[502] in the genus Aggregata[503] living in the
gut cause serious illness in the host. Octopuses have an innate immune
system[504]; their haemocytes[505] locate the foreign invader and attack it
via phagocytosis[506], encapsulation, infiltration, or cytotoxicity[507]. The
haemocytes also contribute to healing injures.[102][508] A gram-negative
bacterium, Vibrio lentus[509], can cause skin lesions, exposure of muscle and
sometimes death.[103][510]
Evolution
---------

The scientific name Octopoda was first given as the order of octopuses in 1818
by English biologist William Elford Leach[511],[104][512] who classified them
as Octopoida the previous year.[2][513] Octopoda consists of around 300 known
species[29][514]: 145  and were historically divided into two suborders,
the Incirrina[515] and the Cirrina.[105][516] More recent evidence suggests
cirrates are the most basal species, not a unique clade[517].[106][518] The
incirrate octopuses (the majority of species) lack the cirri and paired
swimming fins of the cirrates.[39][519] In addition, the internal shell of
incirrates is either present as a pair of stylets[520] or absent altogether.
[107][521]
Fossil history and phylogeny

[Fossil of crown group coleoid on a slab of Jurassic rock from Germany][523]
The octopuses evolved from the Muensterelloidea[524] (fossil pictured) in the
Jurassic[525] period.[108][526] The Cephalopoda descended from a mollusc
resembling the Monoplacophora[527] in the Cambrian[528] some 530 million years
ago. The Coleoidea, which brought their shells inside the body, diverged from
the nautiloids[529] in the Devonian[530] some 416 million years ago. Around
276 million years ago, during the Permian[531], the coleoids split into two
groups, the Vampyropoda[532] and the Decabrachia[533].[109][534] The octopuses
arose from the Muensterelloidea[535] within the Vampyropoda in the
Jurassic[536]. The earliest octopus likely lived near the sea floor (
benthic[537] to demersal[538]) in shallow marine environments.[109][539][
110][540][108][541] Octopuses consist mostly of soft tissue, and so fossils
are relatively rare. As soft-bodied cephalopods, they lack the external shell
of most molluscs, including other cephalopods like the nautiloids and the
(extinct) Ammonoidea[542].[111][543] They have eight limbs like other
Coleoidea[544], but lack the extra specialised feeding appendages known as
tentacles[545] which are longer and thinner with suckers only at their
club-like ends.[112][546][113][547] The vampire squid (Vampyroteuthis[548])
also lacks tentacles but has sensory filaments.[114][549]
The cladograms[550] are based on Sanchez et al., 2018, who created a
molecular phylogeny[551] based on mitochondrial[552] and nuclear DNA[553]
marker sequences.[106][554] The position of the Eledonidae is from Ibáñez et
al., 2020, with a similar methodology.[115][555] Divergence dates are from
Kröger et al., 2011 and Fuchs et al., 2019.[109][556][108][557]

RNA editing and the genome

Octopuses, like other coleoid cephalopods but unlike more basal[628]
cephalopods or other molluscs, are capable of greater RNA editing[629],
changing the nucleic acid sequence[630] of the primary transcript[631] of RNA
molecules, than any other organisms. Much editing is done in the nervous
system, particularly for excitability[632] and neuronal morphology. Coleoids
rely mostly on ADAR[633] enzymes for RNA editing, which requires large,
double-stranded RNA[634] structures. The many editing sites are conserved[635]
in the coleoid genome and the mutation rates for the sites are hampered.
Hence, greater transcriptome plasticity has come at the cost of slower genome
evolution.[116][636]
The genome of octopuses has also gone through several chromosomal fusions and
rearrangements, unlike that of their closest relative the vampire squid, whose
chromosomal structure is more basal and squid-like.[117][637] The octopus
genome is unremarkably bilaterian[638] except for large developments of two
gene families: protocadherins[639], which regulate the development of neurons;
and the C2H2 zinc-finger[640] transcription factors. Many novel genes in both
cephalopods generally and octopus specifically manifest in the animals' skin,
suckers, and nervous system.[45][641]
Relationship to humans
----------------------

Cultural significance

[An ancient nearly spherical vase with 2 handles by the top, painted all over with an octopus decoration in black][643]
Minoan[644] clay vase with octopus decoration, c. 1500 BC Ancient seafaring
people were aware of the octopus, as evidenced by artworks and designs. It was
depicted on coins during the Minoan civilization[645] possibly as early as
1650 BCE and on pottery in Mycenaean Greece[646] around between 1200 and 1100
BCE. A Hawaiian creation myth[647] suggests that the octopus is the lone
survivor of a previous age. The legendary sea monster, the kraken[648] is
conceived as octopus-like.[28][649]: 1, 4–5  Similarly, Medusa[650]
was compared to an octopus, with her snake-hair resembling the creature's
arms.[118][651]: 133  The Akkorokamui[652] is a gigantic octopus-like
monster[653] from Ainu[654] folklore, worshipped in Shinto[655].[119][656]
In the Asuka-era[657] Japanese legend Taishokan, a female diver battles an
octopus to recover a stolen jewel, which became the inspiration for
woodblock printings[658]. Similarly, in the 1973 novel Gravity's Rainbow[659]
an octopus named Grigori attacks a woman on the beach. A battle with an
octopus plays a significant role in Victor Hugo[660]'s 1866 book Travailleurs
de la mer (Toilers of the Sea[661]). The octopus continues to be depicted as
antagonistic in films such as Wake of the Red Witch[662] (1948).[118][663]
: 129–131, 138–139, 145–147 
In political cartoons[664], octopuses have been used to symbolise empires and
large organizations, the arms representing long reach. Octopuses also have an
erotic appeal. Japanese erotic art, shunga[665], includes ukiyo-e[666]
woodblock prints such as Katsushika Hokusai[667]'s 1814 print Tako to ama (
The Dream of the Fisherman's Wife[668]), in which a woman is sexually
intertwined with a large and a small octopus. This art style would inspire
Pablo Picasso[669]'s 1903 drawing An Erotic Drawing: Woman and Octopus.[
118][670]: 126–128  Some individual octopuses gained celebrity status,
notably Paul the Octopus[671] who predicted the winners of the 2010 FIFA
World Cup[672].[28][673]: 3–4 
Danger to humans

[Coloured drawing of a huge octopus rising from the sea and attacking a sailing ship's three masts with its spiralling arms][675]
Pen and wash[676] drawing of an imagined colossal octopus[677] attacking a
ship, by the malacologist[678] Pierre de Montfort[679], 1801 Octopuses
generally avoid humans, but some conflictual incidents have been verified. For
example, a 2.4-metre (8 ft) Pacific octopus, said to be nearly perfectly
camouflaged, "lunged" at a diver and "wrangled" over his camera before it let
go. Another diver recorded the encounter on video.[120][680] All species are
venomous, but only blue-ringed octopuses have venom that is lethal to humans.
[121][681] Blue-ringed octopuses rank amongst the most dangerous marine
animals; their bites are reported each year across the animals' range from
Australia to the eastern Indo-Pacific Ocean. They bite only when provoked or
accidentally touched; bites are small and usually painless. The venom appears
to be able to penetrate the skin without a puncture, given prolonged contact.
It contains tetrodotoxin[682], which causes paralysis by blocking the
transmission of nerve impulses[683] to the muscles. This causes death by
respiratory failure leading to cerebral anoxia[684]. No antidote is known, but
if breathing can be kept going artificially, patients recover within 24 hours.
[122][685][123][686] Bites have been recorded from captive octopuses of other
species; they leave temporary swellings.[30][687]: 68 
As a food source

[image][689] Octopus sushi[690] Octopuses are fished[691] around the world and
between 1988 and 1995, catches varied between 245,320 and 322,999 metric tons.
[124][692] The world catch peaked in 2007 at 380,000 tons, and had fallen by a
tenth by 2012.[125][693] Methods to capture octopuses include pots,
trapping[694], trawling[695], snaring, drift fishing, spearing, hooking and
catching by hands.[124][696] Octopuses are also bycatch[697].[126][698]
Attempts to farm octopuses commercially are controversial.[127][699][128][700]
Octopus is eaten in many cultures, such as those on the Mediterranean and
Asian coasts.[129][701] The arms and other body parts are prepared in ways
that vary by species and geography. Live octopuses or their wriggling pieces
are consumed as san-nakji[702] in Korean cuisine.[130][703][131][704] If not
prepared properly, however, the severed arms can choke the diner with their
suction cups, causing at least one death in 2010.[132][705] Animal welfare
groups have objected to the live consumption of octopuses on the basis that
they can experience pain.[133][706]
Science and technology

In classical Greece, Aristotle[707] (384–322 BC) commented on[708] their
colour-changing abilities, both for camouflage and for signalling[709], in his
Historia animalium[710]: "The octopus ... seeks its prey by so changing its
colour as to render it like the colour of the stones adjacent to it; it does
so also when alarmed[711]."[134][712] Aristotle noted that the octopus had a
hectocotyl arm and suggested it might be used in reproduction. This claim was
widely ignored until the 19th century. It was described in 1829 by the French
zoologist Georges Cuvier[713], who supposed it to be a parasitic worm, naming
it as a new species, Hectocotylus octopodis.[135][714][136][715][137][716]
Other zoologists thought it a spermatophore; the German zoologist
Heinrich Müller[717] believed it was "designed" to detach during copulation.
In 1856, the Danish zoologist Japetus Steenstrup[718] demonstrated that it is
used to transfer sperm, and only rarely detaches.[138][719]
[image][721] Flexible biomimetic[722] 'Octopus' robotics[723] arm. The
BioRobotics Institute, Scuola Superiore Sant'Anna[724], Pisa[725], 2011[
139][726] Octopuses offer many possibilities in biological research[727]; the
California two-spot octopus had its genome sequenced, allowing exploration of
its molecular adaptations.[45][728] Having independently evolved[729]
mammal-like intelligence, octopuses were compared by the philosopher
Peter Godfrey-Smith[730], who studied the nature of intelligence,[140][731] to
hypothetical intelligent extraterrestrials[732].[141][733] Their intelligence
and flexible bodies enable them to escape from supposedly secure tanks in
public aquariums[734].[142][735]
Due to their intelligence, many argue that octopuses should be given
protections when used for experiments.[143][736] In the UK from 1993 to 2012,
the common octopus (Octopus vulgaris) was the only invertebrate protected
under the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986[737].[144][738] In 2012,
this legislation was extended to include all cephalopods[145][739] in
accordance with a general EU[740] directive.[146][741]
Some robotics[742] research is exploring biomimicry[743] of octopus features.
Octopus arms can move and sense largely autonomously without intervention from
the animal's central nervous system. In 2015 a team in Italy built soft-bodied
robots able to crawl and swim, requiring only minimal computation.[147][744]
[148][745] In 2017, a German company made an arm with a soft
pneumatically[746] controlled silicone[747] gripper fitted with two rows of
suckers. It was able to grasp objects such as a metal tube, a magazine, or a
ball, and to fill a glass by pouring water from a bottle.[149][748]
